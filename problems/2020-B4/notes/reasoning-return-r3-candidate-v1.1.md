## F1. Common fixed subgroups carry actual difference sets

Let T be any subgroup of known numerical multipliers fixing D setwise.
Set

    H=G^T={x in G: tx=x for every t in T}, E=D intersect H,
    h=|H|, f=|E|.

For any nonzero z in H, let z=d-e be its unique ordered representation
with d,e in D. For each t in T, z=td-te is a second such representation.
Uniqueness forces td=d and te=e. This holds for every t, so d,e belong
to E. Conversely all differences of E lie in H and remain unique.
Therefore E represents every nonzero element of H exactly once, and

    f(f-1)=h-1.                                          (F1)

If h>1, f>=2, m=f-1>=1, and h=m^2+m+1. E is an actual cyclic
planar difference set of order m in H, not a quotient occupancy vector.
For h=1 the conclusion is only f in{0,1}; this exceptional case must
not be assigned a fictitious nondegenerate subplane. For h=3 one has
m=1, the accepted degenerate order-one difference-set boundary.

If T is generated by integers t1,...,ts modulo v, then

    h=gcd(v,t1-1,...,ts-1).                               (F2)

Indeed H consists of the residues killed by each ti-1; the simultaneous
annihilator in a cyclic group has this size. Knowing that T preserves
D setwise alone supplies the hypotheses, while fixedness of the two
representing points is a consequence of uniqueness. No coprimality
between |T| and v is required.

For a nonidentity T one has h<v, hence m<q when h>1. For m>=2,
the already accepted plane dictionary identifies H with a proper subplane:
the lines meeting H in two points are exactly E+x, x in H. To check this,
the unique line through two points x,y in H has a unique difference pair
in E; its translate parameter lies in H. Its intersection with H is the
corresponding translate of E. Thus this is an embedded subplane.

In particular q>=m^2. Here is the elementary bound rather than an
unmentioned subplane theorem. An external point P belongs to at most
one extended subplane line, since two such lines meet at a subplane
point. If it belongs to one, that line contains m+1 subplane points;
the remaining m^2 subplane points require distinct other lines through
P, giving q+1>=m^2+1. If it belongs to none, all m^2+m+1 subplane
points require distinct lines, a stronger inequality. For m=1 the bound
q>=m^2 is automatic. Consequently every nontrivial proper H also obeys

    h<=q+sqrt(q)+1.                                      (F3)

For the following prime-order conclusion, let t be a known multiplier
of prime order ell and define its OWN fixed subgroup and intersection:

    H_t=G^<t>, h_t=|H_t|, E_t=D intersect H_t, f_t=|E_t|.

Apply F1 specifically to T=<t>. If h_t>1, set m_t=f_t-1,
so h_t=m_t^2+m_t+1. Every t-orbit on D has length1 or ell,
and its length1 points are precisely E_t. Therefore

    q-m_t is divisible by ell when h_t>1.               (F4)

When h_t=1, q+1 is congruent to f_t in{0,1} modulo ell,
where f_t is1 exactly when0 belongs to D. The same actual singleton
membership is retained for every such element. These h_t,f_t,m_t
refer only to <t>. If <t> is contained in a larger chosen T, its
common fixed subgroup H=G^T may be strictly smaller and must NOT
be substituted into this orbit congruence. No prime-order congruence
for |D^T| is asserted merely from the existence of t in T.

These restrictions add fixed-point equations absent from an orbit-size
subset sum alone. They are necessary, not sufficient. Applying these
structural statements recursively to E requires resetting every
hypothesis in its group H, including the actual restricted multiplier
order and that element's own fixed subgroup. E is a genuine subset
with the full unique-difference property; a homomorphic image of D
does not have this property in general.

## F1a. A cofactor obstruction and an explicit restricted family

Write q=pu, where p is any prime divisor of q and u is an integer.
Use the known multiplier p and apply F1 with T=<p>; its
order need not be prime. Its own fixed subgroup has size

    h=gcd(p-1,q^2+q+1)=gcd(p-1,u^2+u+1).                 (F5)

The equality follows simply by reducing p to1 modulo p-1. Thus h must
be1 or m^2+m+1 for an actual cyclic planar order m>=1. If h>1 and
4h-3 is not an odd square, no such D exists. If it is a square, the
result is descent to the indicated smaller order, not an assertion that
that order is a prime power.

In particular, for every prime p congruent to20 modulo57, order q=7p
is impossible. Here u^2+u+1=57=3*19, p-1 is divisible by19 and not
by3, so h=19. But f(f-1)=18 has no integral solution: f=4 gives12
and f=5 gives20. The corresponding n=7p+1 is therefore not taut,
conditional only on the accepted difference-set and multiplier inputs.

This is a nonempty restricted arithmetic family. For example p=761
is prime: division by the primes2,3,5,7,11,13,17,19,23, all the primes
at most sqrt(761)<28, gives respective nonzero remainders1,2,1,5,2,7,
13,1,2. It is20 modulo57, giving q=5327 and n=5328. At this order
q is3 modulo4 and2 modulo3, so the accepted Bruck--Ryser congruence
case and the accepted modulo-three norm case do not themselves apply.
Other published or recorded tests may already exclude this example.
No new finite cutoff, minimal counterexample or independence from every
old test is claimed. No scan was performed. Infinitude of the prime
progression is unnecessary for this assertion and is not imported.

## F2. An involution forces a square: a complete fiber proof

Suppose a known multiplier t has order exactly2 modulo v. Since v is
odd, division by2 is an automorphism of G. Define

    A={x:tx=-x}, B={x:tx=x},
    alpha(x)=(x-tx)/2, beta(x)=(x+tx)/2.

Then alpha,beta are complementary projections, G=A direct_sum B,
and t acts as(a,b)->(-a,b). Let a=|A|, b=|B|. Nonidentity of t
means a>1. For y in B let D_y={x in A:(x,y) in D}. It is symmetric
under x->-x.

A symmetric fiber D_y has at most two points. Indeed if distinct x,z
in it are not opposite, the ordered differences x-z and (-z)-(-x)
are equal and give distinct pairs. Thus any two different members must
be opposites; a third member is impossible. A singleton fiber is {0};
a two-point fiber is {x,-x} with x!=0. Let R be the number of such
two-point fibers. Every nonzero element (z,0) of A is a difference
within one fiber. Each two-point fiber supplies exactly two of these
ordered differences, and no other fiber supplies any. Consequently

    2R=a-1.                                              (F6)

If b=1, D lies in its one symmetric fiber and |D|<=2, contrary to
q>=2. Thus b>1. Apply F1 with T=<t> to B: f=|D intersect B|=s+1 and
b=s^2+s+1 for an integer s>=1. Counting the singleton and double
fibers gives

    q+1=f+2R=s+a, hence a=q-s+1.

Since ab=v,

    (q-s+1)(s^2+s+1)=q^2+q+1,
    (q-s)(q-s^2)=0.                                      (F7)

The root q=s would give a=1 and t identity. Therefore q=s^2, with
s>=2. Put m=s. We have proved

    q=m^2, |A|=m^2-m+1, |B|=m^2+m+1.                    (F8)

These two odd factors of v are coprime: a common divisor divides2m
and is coprime to m. The cyclic group has unique subgroups of these
orders. Modulo |B|, m^3 is1; modulo |A|, m^3 is-1. Thus its action
on A and B agrees with t, and

    t=m^3 modulo v.                                      (F9)

This reconstructs the published Blokhuis--Brouwer--Wilbrink involution
theorem by a direct fiber count. Their applicable original Section2
also proves the result, using the classification of involutory plane
collineations. No such classification is an import in the proof above.
The order of t here is in (Z/vZ)^*, not just its order at one chosen
prime factor.

Consequently, for nonsquare q, every numerical multiplier has odd
order modulo v. An even-order multiplier x would have the involution
x^(ord_v(x)/2), contradicting F8. This is the classical planar Mann
test, not a new theorem. It applies equally to composite v. In
particular an even-order projection of x modulo any divisor of v
already forces a square q, because the full order is then even;
one need not incorrectly identify that involution with global -1.

## F2a. Coherence of the 2-parts when q is square

For q=m^2 the only possible involutory numerical multiplier is m^3.
Every subgroup of numerical multipliers is abelian and has at most
one involution. Its Sylow2 subgroup is therefore cyclic. One elementary
proof of the last fact is induction: in a nontrivial2-group with just
one involution the doubling homomorphism has kernel of size2; its
image has half the order and again at most one involution. Lift a
generator of that cyclic image to obtain an element of full order.

There is an exact local test. Set A0=m^2-m+1, B0=m^2+m+1. For
any even-order known multiplier x write

    ord_v(x)=2^a s, a>=1, s odd,
    a_r=nu_2(ord_(r^e)(x)) for each exact prime power r^e dividing v.

Necessarily

    a_r=a for every r^e dividing A0,
    a_r<a for every r^e dividing B0.                      (F10)

To prove this, the halfway power x^(ord_v(x)/2) equals-1 modulo a
local odd prime power exactly when its local order has full2-part2^a;
otherwise it equals1 there. The nontrivial involution modulo an odd
prime power is uniquely-1, since r^e divides(z-1)(z+1) and the two
factors have gcd at most2. Compare with F9 and the coprime A0,B0
decomposition. In particular inconsistent halfway powers of two known
multipliers are an exclusion, even if each multiplier separately has
a permitted orbit cardinality. F10 is necessary, not a sufficient
certificate that an arbitrary unit is a multiplier or that D exists.

## F3. The relative trace is determined by the fixed set

Continue with a square order and the involution t=m^3. Identify
G=A direct_sum B as above. Let E=D intersect B and let
F=(1/2)E, where division by2 is in B. Let

    P=beta_*(D) in Z[B], U_B=sum_(b in B)b.

P is the projection with multiplicities, not the intersection E.
The exact additional identity is

    P + F*F = 2 U_B.                                    (F11)

Here * is group-ring convolution, not inversion. This is Lemma2 in
the original Blokhuis--Brouwer--Wilbrink paper. The following direct
argument verifies all coefficients and requires no prime-power order.

A planar difference set in an odd group has distinct unordered sums,
including repeated elements: d1+d2=d3+d4 implies the two unordered
pairs agree. If d1!=d3, apply unique ordered differences to
d1-d3=d4-d2; if d1=d3 the remaining equality is immediate.

Each nonfixed orbit {d,td} on D supplies beta(d)=(d+td)/2 to P
with multiplicity2. Each e in E supplies e with multiplicity1. The
convolution F*F supplies e with multiplicity1 for the repeated pair
e,e, and supplies(e+f)/2 with multiplicity2 for each distinct
unordered pair e,f in E. All displayed values from different unordered
pairs of D are different, by the preceding uniqueness. The only overlap
between these two lists is exactly the repeated E pairs, which occur
once in each. Thus all coefficients of P+F*F are either0 or2.
Its augmentation is

    |D|+|E|^2=(m^2+1)+(m+1)^2=2|B|.

It has exactly |B| coefficient positions, proving F11.

Consequently P has value1 exactly on E, value0 exactly on the
binom(m+1,2) distinct averages of two different elements of E, and
value2 at the remaining m(m-1)/2 points. At a nonprincipal complex
character chi of B,

    chi(P)=-chi(F)^2.                                   (F12)

Since E is a planar difference set of order m, |chi(F)|^2=m and
|chi(P)|^2=m^2=q. The accepted quotient norm only gives the last
magnitude. F11 supplies its compatible phases from an actual smaller
fixed difference set; it is stronger structural information than that
magnitude equation alone.

## F3a. The exact remaining lift, not an automatic reconstruction

Let R be the value2 support of P determined by E. Its size is
m(m-1)/2=(|A|-1)/2. There must be a bijection

    R <-> (A minus{0})/{x~-x}.                           (F13)

To see the necessity, every nonzero first-coordinate value in A occurs
in at most one nonfixed fiber. Otherwise two nonfixed points would
have a difference in B, whose unique difference representation must
be between points fixed by this involution, by F1 with T=<t>. F6 and counting show that all values
occur. Thus write f(b) for either representative of the sign pair
assigned to b in R. Then necessarily

    D=({0} cross E) union
      union_(b in R){(f(b),b),(-f(b),b)}.                 (F14)

The arbitrary bijection F13 is not sufficient. Its exact remaining
conditions can be stated without any hidden geometric assertion.
In Z[A] set L_b=1 for b in E, L_b=X^f(b)+X^-f(b) for b in R,
and L_b=0 otherwise. Then F14 is a difference set if and only if

    sum_b L_b L_(b-delta)=U_A for every nonzero delta in B. (F15)

All L_b are invariant under inversion in A. The delta=0 equation
already follows from the sign-pair bijection: it is q*1+U_A by
F6. For nonzero delta, F15 is precisely the coefficient of delta
in DD^(-1)=q*1+U_A U_B. The augmentation of F15 is the old quotient
correlation and follows from F11. Its individual A coefficients are
additional constraints. Thus a quotient or relative trace solution
cannot silently be lifted by selecting an arbitrary bijection.

This exact square-order reformulation overlaps the established function
models discussed in Huang--Schmidt. Their subsequent uniqueness theorems
have additional prime-power hypotheses; none is imported here. No
exhaustive search over F13 or numerical certificate is attempted.

## B1. An explicit barrier to using only positive-definite norms

For every integer q>=2 with k=q+1 and v=q^2+q+1, put
r=sqrt(q), b=(k-r)/v and consider in R[G]

    A=r*1+b*U_G.

It has nonnegative real coefficients, augmentation k, and is fixed
by every numerical multiplier. Since U_G^2=v U_G,

    A A^(-1)=q*1+(2rb+vb^2)U_G=q*1+U_G,                (B1)

because2rb+vb^2=(k^2-q)/v=1. Its nonprincipal character values
all have magnitude sqrt(q). This works even for the excluded q=7p
family above. Its identity coefficient exceeds1 and its other
coefficients are positive, so it is not the characteristic vector of
a subset. It is an exact counterexample to a proposed argument using
only real nonnegativity, the norm, augmentation and multiplier invariance
to force a prime-power order. It does not refute an argument retaining
integer or0/1 coefficients.

In fact retaining integrality with this *full* group-ring identity
and positive augmentation k already recovers0/1 automatically. If
C=sum c_g g has integer coefficients, CC^(-1)=q*1+U_G, and
sum c_g=k, the identity coefficient gives sum c_g^2=k. Thus
sum c_g(c_g-1)=0. Every summand is a nonnegative integer even when
c_g is negative, so each c_g is0 or1. A field-descent output with
different augmentation, only character norms, or a smaller-conductor
identity does not meet these premises. The lifting/integrality gap
must be identified before using such an output as a difference set.
